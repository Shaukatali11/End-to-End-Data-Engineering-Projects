# Data Modeling Patterns: Deep Data Engineering Thinking

## 1. Operational OLTP vs Analytical OLAP Models

### What is the fundamental difference?

The first question you should ask is:

> **“What is this system optimized for—running the business or analyzing the business?”**

An **OLTP system** is designed for operational transactions. Examples are placing an order, updating a customer's address, processing a payment, or changing an inventory quantity.

An **OLAP system** is designed for analytical questions such as:

* What was revenue by region last quarter?
* Which customers purchased more than ₹1 lakh?
* What is the monthly retention rate?
* Which product category is growing fastest?
* What is the average order value by customer segment?

The same business can therefore have two very different models.

### Example

An e-commerce OLTP database might have:

```text
customers
---------
customer_id
name
email
address_id

addresses
---------
address_id
city
state
country

orders
------
order_id
customer_id
order_date
status

order_items
-----------
order_item_id
order_id
product_id
quantity
price

products
--------
product_id
product_name
category_id
```

This is highly normalized.

An analytical model might instead have:

```text
fact_sales
----------
date_key
customer_key
product_key
store_key
order_id
quantity
sales_amount
discount_amount
```

with dimensions:

```text
dim_customer
dim_product
dim_date
dim_store
```

The analytical model is designed so that an analyst can answer questions with relatively simple joins.

### How should you think during an interview?

If the interviewer says:

> "Design the database for an e-commerce application."

You should first ask:

> "Are we designing the operational database or the analytical warehouse?"

That one clarification can completely change your design.

### OLTP priorities

OLTP generally prioritizes:

* fast inserts/updates
* transaction consistency
* concurrency
* referential integrity
* minimizing duplication
* ACID behavior

### OLAP priorities

OLAP generally prioritizes:

* analytical query performance
* aggregations
* historical analysis
* easy querying
* scalable scans
* dimensional relationships
* business-friendly semantics

### Advantages of separating them

One major production principle is:

> **Don't force analytical workloads onto the operational database.**

Imagine your production order database is handling thousands of orders per second while an analyst runs:

```sql
SELECT
    customer_id,
    SUM(order_amount)
FROM orders
GROUP BY customer_id;
```

If the table contains billions of rows, the analytical query could compete with production transactions.

Instead:

```text
Application
    ↓
OLTP Database
    ↓
CDC / ETL / ELT
    ↓
Data Warehouse
    ↓
Dimensional Model
    ↓
BI / Analytics / ML
```

### Interview-level answer

If asked **“Why can't we just use the OLTP database for analytics?”**, don't simply say "performance."

Say:

> "Because OLTP and OLAP have fundamentally different workload characteristics. OLTP optimizes for small, concurrent transactional operations and consistency, while OLAP optimizes for large scans, joins, aggregations, and historical analysis. Running heavy analytical workloads directly against the transactional system can increase CPU, I/O, locking, and latency for business-critical transactions. Therefore, I would generally replicate or transform operational data into an analytical store."

That demonstrates architectural understanding rather than memorization.

# 2. Normalization vs Denormalization

This is one of the most important modeling trade-offs.

### Normalization

Normalization means separating data into logically distinct entities and reducing unnecessary duplication.

For example:

```text
customer
---------
customer_id
customer_name
city_id

city
----
city_id
city_name
state_id

state
-----
state_id
state_name
country_id
```

Instead of storing:

```text
customer_id
customer_name
city
state
country
```

for every customer.

The advantage is consistency.

If the state name changes, you don't need to update millions of customer records.

### Why OLTP likes normalization

Suppose 10 million orders contain:

```text
customer_name
customer_email
customer_city
customer_state
```

You now have huge duplication.

If the customer's email changes, multiple records might need updating.

Normalized design:

```text
customers
   |
   +---- orders
   |
   +---- addresses
```

The customer information exists in one logical location.

### But analytical systems often denormalize

Suppose an analyst wants:

```text
Revenue
GROUP BY country, state, city
```

If the model is excessively normalized, they might need:

```text
fact_sales
   ↓
customer
   ↓
address
   ↓
city
   ↓
state
   ↓
country
```

That increases query complexity.

A dimensional model might provide:

```text
fact_sales
    |
dim_customer
    |
country
state
city
```

or even directly keep commonly required descriptive attributes in `dim_customer`.

### Why denormalization helps analytics

It can provide:

* simpler SQL
* fewer joins
* easier BI development
* potentially better query performance
* easier business interpretation

But there is a cost.

### Disadvantages

Denormalization can cause:

* duplicated data
* larger storage
* update complexity
* inconsistent values
* more complicated ETL
* historical-management challenges

### Interview scenario

Interviewer:

> "You have a customer dimension with country, state, city, and postal code. Should you normalize those into separate tables?"

Good answer:

> "It depends on the access patterns. If analysts frequently filter and group directly by those attributes, keeping them in the customer dimension may provide a simpler star schema. If those attributes have their own complex hierarchies, independent lifecycle, or are shared extensively across multiple dimensions, normalization may make sense. I would avoid normalizing purely because it looks cleaner; the decision should be driven by query patterns, governance, update behavior, and scale."

That is a much stronger answer than:

> "Star schema means denormalization."

### Golden principle

> **Normalize to maintain integrity; denormalize to optimize consumption.**

But remember: this is a guideline, not a law.

# 3. Star Schema vs Snowflake Schema

This is where dimensional modeling becomes practical.

## Star Schema

A star schema has:

```text
                 dim_customer
                      |
                      |
dim_date ---- fact_sales ---- dim_product
                      |
                      |
                  dim_store
```

The fact table is in the center.

Dimensions surround it.

Hence the name **star**.

Example:

```text
fact_sales
----------
date_key
customer_key
product_key
store_key
quantity
sales_amount
discount_amount
```

```text
dim_customer
------------
customer_key
customer_id
customer_name
customer_segment
city
state
country
```

```text
dim_product
-----------
product_key
product_id
product_name
brand
category
subcategory
```

### Why star schemas are popular

They provide:

* simple joins
* intuitive business meaning
* predictable query patterns
* easy BI integration
* good analytical performance
* centralized business definitions

For example:

```sql
SELECT
    p.category,
    SUM(f.sales_amount)
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_product p
    ON f.product_key = p.product_key
GROUP BY p.category;
```

This is easy for analysts to understand.

---

## Snowflake Schema

A snowflake schema normalizes dimensions.

Instead of:

```text
dim_product
-----------
product
brand
subcategory
category
department
```

you might have:

```text
dim_product
     |
dim_subcategory
     |
dim_category
     |
dim_department
```

### Advantages

Snowflake can reduce:

* dimension duplication
* storage
* repeated hierarchical data

It can also make independently managed hierarchies easier to maintain.

### Disadvantages

But queries become more complicated.

Instead of:

```text
fact → product
```

you may have:

```text
fact → product → subcategory → category → department
```

That means more joins.

### When would I choose star?

For a typical BI warehouse:

> **Star schema would usually be my default.**

Because the primary goal is making analytical consumption simple.

### When might snowflake make sense?

Suppose you have a complex enterprise hierarchy:

```text
Product
  ↓
Subcategory
  ↓
Category
  ↓
Business Unit
  ↓
Division
  ↓
Enterprise
```

If those entities have independent ownership, governance, and lifecycle, normalization may be justified.

### Interview question

**Q: Is star schema always better than snowflake?**

No.

A strong answer:

> "No. Star schema is generally preferred for simplicity and analytical usability, but snowflake can be appropriate when dimensions contain large or complex reusable hierarchies where normalization provides meaningful governance, storage, or maintenance benefits. I would decide based on query patterns, dimension size, hierarchy complexity, and ownership rather than applying a universal rule."

### Important communication point

When explaining this to a BI team:

Don't say:

> "I chose star schema because Kimball says so."

Say:

> "The majority of our consumers need product, customer, and date attributes directly alongside sales metrics. A star schema minimizes joins and makes the semantic model easier for analysts to understand."

That connects architecture to business usage.

# 4. Fact Table Types

This is a very important interview area because many candidates know what a fact table is but don't know **which type to choose**.

The three major types are:

1. Transaction fact
2. Periodic snapshot fact
3. Accumulating snapshot fact

---

## 4.1 Transaction Fact

A transaction fact stores one row for each business event.

Example:

```text
fact_order_item
---------------
order_item_key
order_id
customer_key
product_key
date_key
quantity
unit_price
discount
net_sales
```

One order item = one row.

If order 1001 contains:

```text
Laptop
Mouse
Keyboard
```

there could be three rows.

### Grain

The grain is:

> **One row represents one product line item within an order.**

That statement is extremely important.

### When to use

Transaction facts are ideal when the business event itself is important.

Examples:

* sales transaction
* payment
* shipment
* click
* ride
* login
* website event
* support ticket event

### Advantages

* detailed
* flexible
* supports many aggregations
* preserves historical events
* easy to audit

### Disadvantages

* can become extremely large
* analytical queries may require scanning many rows
* late-arriving events need careful handling

---

# 4.2 Periodic Snapshot Fact

A periodic snapshot records the state of something at regular intervals.

Example:

Daily inventory:

```text
fact_inventory_daily
--------------------
date_key
product_key
warehouse_key
inventory_quantity
```

Suppose:

```text
Jan 1 → 100
Jan 2 → 80
Jan 3 → 120
```

Each day gets a record.

### Why not simply use transactions?

Because inventory isn't necessarily an event.

Suppose:

```text
purchase +50
sale -20
return +10
```

You can reconstruct inventory, but sometimes the business needs a direct daily state.

### Examples

* daily account balance
* daily inventory
* monthly subscription status
* daily active users
* daily outstanding loans
* daily headcount

### Grain

> One product at one warehouse for one day.

Again, grain defines everything.

### Advantage

Very easy to answer:

> "What was inventory on March 15?"

### Disadvantage

Storage can grow rapidly.

If you have:

```text
10 million products
×
1,000 warehouses
×
365 days
```

the potential row count becomes enormous.

---

# 4.3 Accumulating Snapshot

This represents a business process that progresses through stages.

For example, an order lifecycle:

```text
Order Created
      ↓
Payment Confirmed
      ↓
Packed
      ↓
Shipped
      ↓
Delivered
```

You could model:

```text
fact_order_lifecycle
---------------------
order_id
created_date
payment_date
packed_date
shipped_date
delivered_date
```

The same row gets updated as the process progresses.

### This is fundamentally different from transaction fact.

Transaction fact:

> "Something happened."

Accumulating snapshot:

> "Where is this business process currently?"

### Examples

* loan application
* insurance claim
* order fulfillment
* recruitment pipeline
* hospital admission
* shipping lifecycle

### Advantages

Excellent for process analytics.

You can calculate:

```text
shipping_duration
payment_to_shipping
order_to_delivery
```

### Disadvantages

It requires updates.

That means your data pipeline needs to handle:

* updates
* late-arriving events
* missing milestones
* corrections

### Interview decision framework

Ask:

> Is this an event?

Use **transaction fact**.

> Do I need state at regular intervals?

Use **periodic snapshot**.

> Does one business process move through known milestones?

Use **accumulating snapshot**.

That simple decision tree is extremely useful in interviews.

# 5. Slowly Changing Dimensions — SCD

This is one of the most frequently asked data modeling interview topics.

The core question is:

> **What should happen when a dimension attribute changes?**

Suppose:

```text
Customer:
ID = 101
Name = Rahul
City = Hyderabad
Segment = Silver
```

Tomorrow:

```text
City = Bangalore
Segment = Gold
```

What should your warehouse do?

That depends on the business requirement.

---

# SCD Type 1 — Overwrite

Old value is replaced.

Before:

```text
customer_id | city
101         | Hyderabad
```

After:

```text
customer_id | city
101         | Bangalore
```

History is lost.

### Use when

You don't care about historical values.

Examples:

* correcting spelling
* correcting invalid email
* fixing bad data
* current customer status where historical status isn't required

### Advantage

Very simple.

### Disadvantage

Historical analysis becomes impossible.

If the business asks:

> "How many customers were in Hyderabad last year?"

you cannot accurately answer using the overwritten dimension.

---

# SCD Type 2 — Preserve History

This is much more powerful.

Instead of updating the row, create a new version.

```text
customer_sk | customer_id | city       | start_date | end_date   | current
1           | 101         | Hyderabad  | 2025-01-01 | 2026-04-10 | N
2           | 101         | Bangalore  | 2026-04-11 | NULL       | Y
```

Here:

* `customer_id` = business key
* `customer_sk` = surrogate key

### Why surrogate key matters

Suppose:

```text
2025 sale → Hyderabad customer
2026 sale → Bangalore customer
```

The fact rows should point to the appropriate dimension version.

```text
fact_sales
----------
sale_date
customer_sk
amount
```

2025 sales:

```text
customer_sk = 1
```

2026 sales:

```text
customer_sk = 2
```

Now historical reporting remains correct.

### Example question

> "What was revenue from customers belonging to the Hyderabad segment at the time of purchase?"

SCD Type 2 allows you to answer this.

### Advantages

* complete history
* point-in-time reporting
* auditability
* historical segmentation

### Disadvantages

* more rows
* more ETL complexity
* surrogate-key management
* more complicated joins
* late-arriving dimension challenges

---

# SCD Type 3 — Limited History

Instead of keeping unlimited versions, keep the current and previous value.

Example:

```text
customer_id
current_city
previous_city
```

Before:

```text
current_city = Hyderabad
previous_city = NULL
```

After moving:

```text
current_city = Bangalore
previous_city = Hyderabad
```

### Advantage

Simple and useful when only limited history is required.

### Disadvantage

If the customer moves:

```text
Hyderabad
↓
Bangalore
↓
Mumbai
↓
Delhi
```

you lose older history.

### Interview decision

Ask:

> "Does the business need historical reporting?"

If no:

**Type 1**

If complete history is required:

**Type 2**

If only limited previous-state comparison is required:

**Type 3**

### Strong interview statement

> "I don't choose SCD Type 2 just because it's more sophisticated. I first identify whether historical attribute values affect business reporting. If historical context matters, Type 2 is appropriate; otherwise Type 1 reduces complexity."

That's the kind of reasoning interviewers want.

# 6. Data Vault Modeling

Data Vault becomes relevant when the organization has:

* many source systems
* changing source structures
* complex enterprise integration
* strong audit requirements
* long-term historical tracking
* multiple business domains

Data Vault separates the model into:

```text
Hubs
Links
Satellites
```

---

## Hub

A hub represents a core business entity.

Examples:

```text
HUB_CUSTOMER
------------
customer_hk
customer_id
load_date
record_source
```

The hub contains the business key and metadata.

---

## Link

A link represents a relationship.

For example:

```text
customer ↔ account
```

```text
LINK_CUSTOMER_ACCOUNT
---------------------
customer_hk
account_hk
load_date
record_source
```

---

## Satellite

A satellite stores descriptive attributes and their history.

```text
SAT_CUSTOMER
------------
customer_hk
customer_name
email
city
customer_segment
effective_date
load_date
record_source
```

### Why this architecture exists

Imagine an enterprise has:

```text
CRM
ERP
Billing
Mobile App
Legacy System
Partner System
```

All systems might define customers differently.

Data Vault can preserve:

* source history
* relationships
* changes
* lineage
* raw integration structure

### Advantages

* highly auditable
* flexible
* scalable for multiple sources
* strong historical tracking
* separates business keys from descriptive attributes

### Disadvantages

The biggest disadvantage:

> **It can be complex for analytical consumers.**

A simple business question might require many joins across hubs, links, and satellites.

Therefore, Data Vault often isn't the final BI-facing model.

A common architecture is:

```text
Source Systems
      ↓
Data Vault
      ↓
Business Transformations
      ↓
Dimensional / Semantic Model
      ↓
BI
```

### Interview question

**"Would you choose Data Vault or Star Schema?"**

Don't answer:

> "Data Vault is better."

Instead:

> "They solve different problems. Data Vault is useful for enterprise integration, auditability, and historical source preservation, while star schemas are generally better for analytical consumption. If I have many heterogeneous source systems and changing requirements, Data Vault may be valuable as an integration layer. I would still consider exposing dimensional or semantic models to BI consumers."

This demonstrates architectural maturity.

---

# 7. Grain — The Single Most Important Modeling Decision

If you remember only one concept from this entire section, remember **grain**.

### Grain means:

> **What exactly does one row represent?**

Before creating a fact table, you should be able to complete this sentence:

> "One row in this table represents ______."

If you cannot complete that sentence precisely, the model isn't ready.

---

## Example

Suppose you create:

```text
fact_sales
```

What does one row represent?

Possibilities:

### Option 1

One row = one order.

```text
order_id
customer_id
order_total
```

### Option 2

One row = one order item.

```text
order_id
product_id
quantity
unit_price
```

These are not equivalent.

Suppose:

```text
Order 1001

Laptop = ₹50,000
Mouse  = ₹2,000
```

Order-level grain:

```text
1001 | 52,000
```

Order-item grain:

```text
1001 | Laptop | 50,000
1001 | Mouse  | 2,000
```

If you mix these grains, you can create serious aggregation errors.

---

## Classic interview trap

Suppose you have:

```text
fact_orders
```

with:

```text
order_id
order_total
```

and:

```text
fact_order_items
```

with:

```text
order_id
product_id
item_amount
```

You join them:

```sql
SELECT
    SUM(o.order_total)
FROM fact_orders o
JOIN fact_order_items i
    ON o.order_id = i.order_id;
```

If one order has five items, `order_total` gets repeated five times.

You have accidentally multiplied the measure.

This is a **grain mismatch**.

### Correct approach

Aggregate item-level data first:

```sql
SELECT
    order_id,
    SUM(item_amount)
FROM fact_order_items
GROUP BY order_id;
```

or use the appropriate fact table directly.

### Grain also determines dimensions

If grain is:

> One order item

Then product is meaningful.

If grain is:

> One order

Then product may not have a single value.

That means grain influences:

* columns
* keys
* dimensions
* measures
* joins
* aggregation
* partitioning
* data quality tests
* downstream semantics

### Interview communication

Before proposing tables, say:

> "Before defining the schema, I want to establish the grain because that determines what each row means and prevents double-counting or ambiguous relationships."

That immediately signals strong modeling knowledge.

# 8. Denormalization and Semi-Structured Data Modeling

Modern data engineering isn't only about traditional relational tables.

We also deal with:

```json
{
  "customer_id": 101,
  "name": "Rahul",
  "preferences": {
    "language": "English",
    "notifications": true
  },
  "devices": [
    {
      "type": "mobile",
      "os": "Android"
    }
  ]
}
```

Now the modeling question becomes:

> **Which fields should become relational columns, which should remain nested, and which should be extracted into separate structures?**

---

## Option 1 — Flatten everything

You could create:

```text
customer_id
name
language
notifications
device_type
device_os
```

This is easy for SQL consumers.

But arrays create complications.

If a customer has:

```text
3 devices
```

you might create:

```text
customer_id | device
101         | mobile
101         | laptop
101         | tablet
```

Now you must clearly define the grain.

---

## Option 2 — Keep nested structures

Some analytical platforms support:

```text
customer_id
name
preferences
devices[]
```

This can preserve the original structure.

### Advantages

* preserves source structure
* less transformation
* useful for flexible attributes
* good for semi-structured ingestion

### Disadvantages

* SQL becomes more complex
* BI tools may struggle
* joins and aggregations can become harder
* schema governance becomes more difficult

---

## Option 3 — Hybrid approach

Often this is the best production choice.

Keep frequently queried attributes as columns:

```text
customer_id
name
country
customer_segment
```

while keeping less frequently used flexible attributes nested:

```text
preferences
metadata
device_details
```

Then flatten/extract only when the business requires it.

### Example

Suppose an application sends:

```json
{
  "customer_id": 1001,
  "country": "India",
  "preferences": {
      "language": "English",
      "theme": "dark"
  },
  "metadata": {
      "campaign": "summer_sale",
      "source": "mobile"
  }
}
```

For analytics:

```text
dim_customer
------------
customer_id
country
language
theme
```

Maybe campaign information belongs elsewhere depending on the business grain.

### Important question

Don't blindly flatten everything.

Ask:

> "How will this field be queried?"

If analysts constantly filter:

```sql
WHERE country = 'India'
```

make it a proper column.

If a nested attribute is rarely accessed and changes frequently, keeping it semi-structured may be reasonable.

# How All 8 Concepts Connect

The biggest mistake is learning these as separate definitions.

In a real project, they form a decision chain.

Suppose the business says:

> "Build an analytics warehouse for an e-commerce platform."

You should think:

```text
1. Understand business questions
             ↓
2. Identify OLTP source systems
             ↓
3. Determine analytical workload
             ↓
4. Identify business processes
             ↓
5. Define grain
             ↓
6. Identify facts
             ↓
7. Identify dimensions
             ↓
8. Determine historical requirements
             ↓
9. Choose SCD strategy
             ↓
10. Decide star/snowflake
             ↓
11. Decide normalization/denormalization
             ↓
12. Handle semi-structured attributes
             ↓
13. Add performance strategy
             ↓
14. Define data quality rules
             ↓
15. Define consumption/semantic layer
```

That's the actual **data modeling thought process**.

---

# The Most Important Interview Framework

When an interviewer gives you a modeling problem, don't immediately start drawing tables.

Use this sequence:

## Step 1 — Clarify the business

Ask:

> What decisions should this model support?

Example:

> "Are we primarily analyzing revenue, customer behavior, inventory, or order fulfillment?"

---

## Step 2 — Identify business processes

For e-commerce:

```text
Customer acquisition
Order
Payment
Shipment
Delivery
Return
Inventory
```

Each process may require a different fact.

---

## Step 3 — Define grain

For example:

> "For sales, I will model one row per order line item."

This is foundational.

---

## Step 4 — Identify facts

```text
fact_sales
fact_payment
fact_shipment
fact_inventory_daily
```

---

## Step 5 — Identify dimensions

```text
dim_customer
dim_product
dim_date
dim_store
dim_location
```

---

## Step 6 — Determine history

Ask:

> "If customer segment changes from Silver to Gold, do historical sales need to remain associated with the old segment?"

If yes:

```text
SCD Type 2
```

---

## Step 7 — Choose schema pattern

Default analytical design:

```text
Star Schema
```

unless there is a specific reason to normalize.

---

## Step 8 — Consider performance

Ask:

* How many rows?
* What is the query frequency?
* What columns are filtered?
* What dimensions are commonly joined?
* Do we need aggregates?
* Should we partition/cluster?
* Are there extremely high-cardinality dimensions?

---

## Step 9 — Define data quality

For example:

```text
fact_sales.order_id should not be NULL
quantity >= 0
sales_amount >= 0
customer_key should resolve
product_key should resolve
```

And most importantly:

> **Grain uniqueness must be tested.**

If grain is:

> one order line

then perhaps:

```text
(order_id, order_line_id)
```

must be unique.

---

# How You Should Communicate With Different Teams

This is extremely important for a senior data engineer.

You don't explain the same model to every team in the same language.

### With Business/Product

Talk about:

* business definitions
* metrics
* reporting requirements
* historical behavior
* KPIs

Example:

> "Do you want revenue based on order creation date, payment date, or shipment date?"

That's a modeling question with business consequences.

---

### With Backend Engineers

Talk about:

* source tables
* primary keys
* foreign keys
* CDC
* update behavior
* event ordering
* schema changes

Example:

> "Is `order_id` globally unique, and does the source guarantee that updates arrive in order?"

---

### With Analytics/BI Team

Talk about:

* dimensions
* measures
* grain
* joins
* metric definitions
* filtering
* dashboard performance

Example:

> "The sales fact is at order-line grain, so `order_total` should not be summed directly from this table."

---

### With ML Team

Talk about:

* point-in-time correctness
* leakage
* feature history
* event timestamps
* slowly changing attributes
* reproducibility

For example, if you're creating a customer feature:

```text
customer_segment
```

you need to ensure the feature represents the value **available at prediction time**, not a future value.

That's a data modeling issue too.

---

### With Data Platform/Infrastructure Team

Talk about:

* storage
* partitioning
* clustering
* compute
* retention
* workload
* scalability
* cost

# A Real Interview Example

Imagine the interviewer says:

> **"Design a data model for Uber-like ride analytics."**

Don't immediately say:

```text
fact_rides
dim_driver
dim_customer
```

First think.

### Business questions

They might ask:

* rides per city
* revenue per driver
* average trip duration
* cancellation rate
* driver utilization
* rider retention

### Business process

Ride lifecycle:

```text
Requested
   ↓
Driver Assigned
   ↓
Driver Arrived
   ↓
Trip Started
   ↓
Trip Completed
```

### Transaction fact

Potential grain:

> One row per completed ride.

```text
fact_ride
---------
ride_key
ride_id
rider_key
driver_key
pickup_location_key
dropoff_location_key
request_date_key
completion_date_key
distance
duration
fare
surge_amount
```

### Dimensions

```text
dim_rider
dim_driver
dim_location
dim_date
dim_vehicle
```

### But now interviewer asks:

> "What if driver information changes?"

You think:

```text
SCD Type 2
```

if historical driver attributes matter.

Then:

> "What if we need to analyze driver status every day?"

You may introduce:

```text
fact_driver_daily_snapshot
```

Then:

> "What if we need to analyze each stage of the ride?"

Now you may need event-level data:

```text
fact_ride_event
```

with grain:

> one ride lifecycle event.

Now the model has evolved because **business questions determined the model**.

That is exactly how you should approach data modeling.

---

# The Golden Rules You Should Remember

### Rule 1

> **Start with business questions, not tables.**

### Rule 2

> **Define grain before designing the fact table.**

### Rule 3

> **Never mix grains casually.**

### Rule 4

> **Facts represent measurable business processes/events; dimensions provide descriptive context.**

### Rule 5

> **Choose SCD behavior based on historical requirements.**

### Rule 6

> **Star schema is generally optimized for analytical usability, not theoretical normalization purity.**

### Rule 7

> **Denormalization is a trade-off, not automatically good or bad.**

### Rule 8

> **A data model should make correct SQL easy and incorrect SQL difficult.**

### Rule 9

> **The model is a contract between data producers and consumers.**

### Rule 10

> **Performance comes after correctness, but the model should be designed with workload characteristics in mind.**

# What Interviewers Are Actually Testing

When they ask:

> "Design a sales data model."

They aren't only checking whether you know `fact_sales` and `dim_product`.

They're evaluating whether you can answer:

```text
What does one row mean?
        ↓
What business process does it represent?
        ↓
What are the dimensions?
        ↓
What are the measures?
        ↓
What happens when dimensions change?
        ↓
What history must be preserved?
        ↓
How will users query it?
        ↓
Can joins cause duplication?
        ↓
How will we validate it?
        ↓
How will it scale?
        ↓
How will we communicate the model?
